## 1. Systems thinking

Systems thinking studies how actors, resources, incentives, and feedback loops influence one another over time. A useful analysis starts by defining the system boundary, then checks whether an apparent improvement shifts cost or harm somewhere outside that boundary.

A feedback loop exists when an output of a system later becomes an input to that same system and influences future behaviour. Because effects can be delayed, a decision that improves one metric today can create an unintended consequence later.

### Example

A company replaces lightweight plastic packaging with heavier glass. Packaging waste falls, but transport emissions rise because every shipment is heavier. The decision should be assessed across both material waste and logistics emissions.

### Common trap

Optimising one visible metric while ignoring effects elsewhere in the system.

## 2. Stakeholder value

A stakeholder is a person or group that can affect a decision or be affected by it. Stakeholder analysis distinguishes **impact** from **influence**:

- impact asks how strongly the decision affects the stakeholder;
- influence asks how strongly the stakeholder can change the decision or outcome.

Material interests are interests significant enough to affect the decision. Materiality does not depend only on group size or on how easy an effect is to measure.

### Example

A supplier-code policy may reduce operational risk for a company while increasing compliance costs for small suppliers. The company should examine who carries the cost, who receives the benefit, and who can influence implementation.

### Common trap

Treating every stakeholder as equally influential or assuming that influence and impact are the same.

## 3. Circular economy

A circular economy aims to keep products, components, and materials useful for longer. Preferred strategies include durability, maintenance, repair, reuse, remanufacturing, and—after higher-value options are exhausted—recycling.

Repair and reuse usually preserve more embedded value than material recycling because the product, its components, and the energy already invested in manufacturing remain useful. Modular design, replaceable parts, and durable materials make those strategies easier.

### Example

A furniture company leases office chairs, repairs returned units, and reuses components. Retaining ownership gives the company an incentive to recover and refurbish products.

### Common trap

Equating circularity only with recycling at the end of a product's life.

## 4. Carbon accounting basics

Carbon accounting commonly groups emissions into three scopes:

- **Scope 1:** direct emissions from sources an organisation owns or controls, such as fuel burned in company vehicles;
- **Scope 2:** indirect emissions associated with purchased electricity, steam, heating, or cooling;
- **Scope 3:** other value-chain emissions, including many supplier, logistics, employee travel, product-use, and end-of-life activities.

The largest source is not always the one a company controls directly. The three-scope structure helps locate material emissions and choose relevant actions.

### Common trap

Assuming direct operational emissions are automatically the organisation's largest source.
